# Why websites built remotely abroad do not sell in Slovakia

Let me be clear from the start, to avoid a misunderstanding: **this is not a complaint about quality of work.** There are strong developers and designers abroad, and many of them build things better than the average local contractor. It is not about craft.

It is that a website does not sell through code or design. A website sells through **matching the expectations of a specific buyer** — and the Slovak buyer differs from others more than you would think.

Below are seven specific differences, each of which costs enquiries.

## 1. Mandatory company details nobody abroad knows about

A Slovak company must state on its website the name exactly as in the register, the registered address, IČO, DIČ and the entry in the Commercial Register — court, section and insert number.

A remote studio usually puts a name and a phone number in the footer. Formally that is a breach. Practically, a Slovak buyer used to seeing an IČO reads its absence as "unclear who these people are". Especially where a deposit is involved.

The full list is in [the mandatory details on a website](/en/blog/mandatory-website-details/).

## 2. The wrong payment methods

Sites built remotely often carry a set of payment methods normal for another market. A Slovak buyer expects card payment, Apple Pay and Google Pay, payment through their own banking app and a QR code for a transfer.

Separately: **from 1 May 2026 a seller must make cashless payment possible** on any purchase above one euro. A studio in another country does not know about that requirement simply because it does not exist at home. Details in [accepting card payments](/en/blog/accept-card-payments/).

## 3. A cookie banner that does not meet the rules

European consent requirements are stricter than in most neighbouring countries outside the EU: refusing must be as easy as accepting, categories must be separated, nothing loads before consent.

The typical banner with a single "Accept" button does not meet that. And without Consent Mode v2 the advertising breaks. [Analysis here](/en/blog/cookie-banner-2026/).

## 4. Local search is not accounted for

A Slovak buyer searches in Slovak, and the queries are their own. A word-for-word translation of Russian or English phrasing produces combinations nobody types.

Local platforms also matter noticeably here — Firmy.sk, Azet, Bazoš — along with the Google Business Profile. For a local business the Google listing often brings people before the site has gained any positions. Remote studios do not deal with any of this, because for them that world does not exist.

## 5. A tone that does not work here

A subtle thing, but a visible one. The devices common on some markets — superlatives, "we are the leaders", "over 1,000 satisfied clients", aggressive countdown timers and pop-ups — provoke distrust rather than interest in a Slovak buyer.

Restraint works better here: specifics instead of promises, real examples instead of "a wide range of services", clear prices instead of "from 1 €". A site translated along with the intonation of the original reads as foreign.

## 6. Nobody can come to you

For some clients this is decisive. The ability to meet in Bratislava, look each other in the eye and discuss the project across a table is something a remote contractor will never provide.

The same applies to time zones during an outage. When a site goes down at ten on a Wednesday morning, it matters that the person fixing it works the same hours you do.

## 7. An invoice your accountant cannot process

A practical point, usually discovered at the end. A Slovak company with an IČO issues a faktúra you put through your expenses and reclaim DPH on. An invoice from a foreign supplier is a different story for accounting: reverse charge, extra filings, and sometimes an accountant who simply refuses to take it.

The difference in hourly rate that motivated the whole exercise is often eaten right here.

## What follows from this

Not that remote work is impossible. Many tasks are solved perfectly well at a distance, and I work with clients in other cities online myself.

Something else follows: **a site meant to sell on the Slovak market has to be built with an understanding of that market.** This is not about the language of the interface. It is about which data the buyer expects to see, what they are used to paying with, where they look for you and what they treat as a sign of a reliable company.

A technically flawless site that does not answer those questions simply brings no enquiries. The galling part is that the cause is invisible: the site is handsome, fast, everything works — and the phone does not ring.

## How to check your own site

1. Are the IČO, DIČ and register entry in the footer?
2. Can people pay by card and through a banking app?
3. Is refusing cookies as accessible as accepting?
4. Which Slovak queries does the site appear for at all?
5. Is the Google Business Profile filled in?
6. Does the text read as written here — or as translated?

If at least three items do not check out, the problem is not the design.
