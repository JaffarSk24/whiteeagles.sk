# Do you need a website in Slovak if all your clients speak Russian

The owner's logic is clear enough: the clients speak Russian, business is conducted in Russian, so why pay for a second version. Besides, it can always be translated later.

The logic holds right up until you start counting what is actually being lost. Point by point below — and at the end, when a Slovak version genuinely is not needed.

## The Russian-speaking market in Slovakia is small

Starting with the unpleasant but honest part. A Russian-speaking audience in Slovakia exists and it has money, but by search volume it is several times smaller than the Slovak one.

Which means something simple: **the Russian version serves the people who already found you. The Slovak one brings new ones.** While you work on recommendations inside the community the difference is invisible. The moment you start growing, you hit a ceiling that did not have to be there.

## Your clients are not isolated

Even if your buyer speaks Russian, the environment around them is Slovak.

**They show your site to partners and acquaintances**, and some of those are Slovaks. A site they cannot read is a recommendation cut short.

**You have Slovak suppliers, landlords, contractors.** They also look at your site when deciding whether to work with you.

**A bank, an authority, an insurer** open the site too when checking a company. The absence of a Slovak version reads here as "this firm is not quite from here".

## Trust works differently than you would think

There is a non-obvious effect: **a Slovak version raises trust even among Russian-speaking clients.**

The reason is simple. A company with a site in Slovak looks rooted — it works here rather than "trying it for now". A company with only a Russian version reads as temporary, even at ten years old.

For someone deciding who to hand a deposit to, that is a tangible difference.

## What is lost in search

A technical but important layer.

Slovak queries are their own body of words, not a translation of Russian ones. People search for `tvorba webstránok`, `oprava webstránky`, `seo audit webu`. If those phrasings appear nowhere on your site, you never show up for them — regardless of how good the service is.

And the reverse: a Russian-language version with no geographic anchor competes with the entire Russian-language internet and usually loses. Russian content makes sense when it is **about Slovakia**: s.r.o., DPH, eKasa, local rules. That is exactly where there is no competition and exactly where you get found.

## When a Slovak version genuinely is not needed

Honestly, such cases exist.

**You work only with the diaspora and do not plan to grow.** A narrow service for a specific community, where clients come by recommendation.

**Your business is not physically in Slovakia** and the company is merely registered here.

**You service one or two corporate contracts** and the site is a business card, not a source of enquiries.

In every other case the second version pays for itself — and the earlier it is built, the cheaper it is.

## How to build a bilingual site properly

There are plenty of technical mistakes here that devalue the whole effort.

**Each language on its own address.** `/sk/` and `/ru/`, not a switch that substitutes text inside one page. The search engine must see two separate pages.

**Hreflang markup between the versions.** It tells the search engine these are not duplicates but language variants of the same material, and which version to show to whom. Without it Google chooses for itself, often badly.

**Language detection on arrival.** A visitor with a Slovak system should land on the Slovak version, one with a Russian system on the Russian. The switch must stay, though: the choice always belongs to the person.

**Not machine translation.** A Slovak reader spots it instantly, and trust falls further than if the version did not exist.

**Texts, not calques.** Good Slovak copy is not a sentence-by-sentence translation of the Russian but the same meaning said the way it is said here.

## What it costs

A second version usually adds no more than a third to the project: structure, layout, functionality and settings are shared, only the content differs.

The other route costs more — build one version and add the second a year later. By then articles, service pages and products have accumulated and everything has to be translated at once. Plus the technical side, never designed for two languages, has to be rebuilt.

## What to do

1. Decide which version is the main one. Usually Slovak — it matches the market the business is registered in and covers more queries.
2. Check that each version has its own address and that they are linked by hreflang.
3. Check where a visitor with a Slovak system lands.
4. Do not machine-translate anything that is supposed to sell.
5. Make the Russian content **about Slovakia** — there is no competition there.
