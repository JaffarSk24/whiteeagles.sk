# Web design for business: what good design must do and what it costs

Web design discussions usually turn on whether people like it. A company website is not a picture on a wall, though. Its job is to make a visitor understand within seconds what you offer and to whom, trust you and send an enquiry. Design that helps with that is good even if it wins no awards. Design that gets in the way is bad even if it is beautiful.

Below: what good website design for a business must do, how the work goes, when a redesign is due and what it costs. If you want the website straight away, see [web development](/en/service/webdev/): design and development on custom code, a landing page from 1,500€.

## What good web design must do

**The first screen says everything that matters.** What you do, for whom, where, and what the visitor should do next. If someone cannot find that without scrolling, they go back to the next Google result.

**Structure by service, not by org chart.** Each service has its own page with a price, the process and answers to questions. Ads and search then take people exactly where the answer is.

**Trust.** Real photos instead of stock, prices or at least ranges, references, company details with the company ID. Slovaks check a supplier thoroughly; more on that in [how Slovaks choose a contractor](/en/blog/how-slovaks-choose-contractor/).

**The phone as the main screen.** More than half of all visits come from mobile. The design is made for the phone and then widened for the desktop, not the other way round. Buttons must be big enough for a thumb and the form fillable with one hand.

**Speed.** Every second of loading costs part of the visitors, and Google takes it into account. Heavy animations and five-megabyte images are design decisions with a price.

**Readability.** Enough text contrast, a sensible font size, short paragraphs and headings you can scan the page by. Most people do not read text; they scan it.

**One clear call to action.** On every page it is obvious what to do next: send an enquiry, call, book. Five equally loud buttons amount to none.

## Custom design or a template

A template is a cheap start, but it decides for you how the company will look, what the site can do and how many plugins will need updating. Hundreds of other companies have the same theme.

Custom design starts from the structure of your services and from what the visitor should do. I build websites on custom code, without WordPress or site builders, so the design is not limited by what a theme allows. How to decide in general is covered in [WordPress or custom](/en/blog/wordpress-or-custom/), and when a free site builder is enough in [free website builders versus a custom site](/en/blog/free-website-builder-vs-custom/).

## How the work goes

1. **A conversation**: which services, who the customers are, what the site should bring. No forty-page brief needed.
2. **Structure**: which pages, what goes on the first screen, where the buttons lead.
3. **Design** of the first screen and the key pages, phone first.
4. **Development** on custom code with forms, enquiry tracking, a cookie banner and the mandatory company details.
5. **Checks** on phones and desktops, speed, a test enquiry.

On average I deliver a site in 10 working days, 95% of projects within 15.

## When a website needs a redesign

- it is awkward to use or falls apart on a phone;
- it loads slowly;
- it looks dated next to the competitors a customer opens right after you;
- the first screen does not make clear what you offer;
- it has traffic but no enquiries.

In a redesign **page addresses are kept or redirected**, and titles and structured data carried over, so the site does not lose its Google rankings. I bill redesigns of existing sites at 35€/hr by scope. If nearly everything has to change, a new site works out better.
