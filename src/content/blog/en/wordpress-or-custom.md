# WordPress or a custom build: choosing by the brief, not by fashion

The argument is old and mostly unhelpful, because each side answers a different question. A WordPress developer counts the launch budget. A framework developer counts speed and control. Both are right and both are talking about something other than what matters.

What actually decides it is different: **what you will be doing with the site in a year.** Here it is factually, without campaigning for either side.

## What is being compared

First, definitions, because "custom" means different things to different people.

**WordPress** is a content management system running more than 40 % of all websites in the world. You get a ready system, a theme (the look) and plugins (the functions).

**A custom build** is a site written for a specific brief on modern tools: React, Next.js, Python or PHP. There is no ready system; there is exactly the functionality required.

Important: "custom" does not mean "no admin panel". A panel is built — just for the sections that genuinely change.

## Money: do not count the first invoice

The most common mistake is comparing the launch price.

| | WordPress | Custom |
|---|---|---|
| **Launch** | cheaper | dearer |
| **Plugins and subscriptions** | 100–600 € a year | usually none |
| **Maintenance** | mandatory and regular | minimal |
| **Changes** | partly yourself, partly a developer | a developer |
| **After 3 years** | often a rebuild | still working |

The rebuild line is the key one. A template site lives until the first task the template cannot do. Then it begins: one plugin almost fits but not quite; a second conflicts with the first; a theme edit disappears with the next update. At some point it becomes cheaper to build again from scratch.

## Speed: the myth and the truth

The truth: a typical WordPress site loads slowly.

The myth: WordPress is to blame.

What makes it slow is a specific build. A marketplace theme drags sliders, a page builder and a dozen plugins along with it, each with its own set of scripts and styles. Together, megabytes of code load for a page with one paragraph of text.

A carefully assembled WordPress — a light theme, no page builder, three plugins rather than fifteen — runs fast. But such a build takes discipline and costs noticeably more than "we bought a theme for 60 dollars".

Why it matters: **Google factors speed into positions and judges by the mobile version.** How to check your own — [the DIY SEO audit](/en/blog/seo-audit-yourself/).

## Security: where the hole actually is

They do not attack WordPress. They attack an un-updated plugin.

The mechanics are almost always the same: a vulnerability is found in a popular plugin, the author releases an update, and bots sweep the internet looking for those who did not apply it. A week after disclosure the only vulnerable sites are the ones where nobody did anything.

The practical conclusion follows. A WordPress site requires regular maintenance — not an optional service but part of the cost of ownership. If there is nobody to do it, the question is not whether it will be compromised but when.

A custom site has a smaller attack surface simply because there is almost no third-party code in it.

## Slovak specifics that general comparisons omit

Here WordPress and a custom build fail equally easily — just differently.

**Mandatory company details.** A Slovak company's site must state the IČO, DIČ, registered address and register entry. No international theme accounts for that: it was not written for Slovakia. [The full list](/en/blog/mandatory-website-details/).

**A cookie banner with Consent Mode v2.** Ready plugins charge a subscription and often block measurement more than needed — you lose data even where consent was given. [Analysis](/en/blog/cookie-banner-2026/).

**Enquiry measurement.** A marketplace theme will not configure GA4 with key events for you. Without that there is no way to know whether the site works. [How to set it up](/en/blog/ga4-setup/).

**Language.** Multilingual support in WordPress is handled by a plugin, and its choice decides whether Google sees your language versions as versions or as duplicates. [Why that matters](/en/blog/do-you-need-slovak-website/).

## When WordPress is the right choice

Without irony — there are plenty of such cases:

- **A content site** whose point is regular publishing: a blog, a magazine, a news portal.
- **A small site on a limited budget**, where you need to announce yourself quickly.
- **There is a person who will run the site** and needs a familiar editor.
- **A standard brief with no non-standard requirements**: services, prices, contacts, a form.

## When a custom solution makes sense

- **There is logic ready plugins do not have**: booking by your own rules, price calculation, integration with an accounting system.
- **Speed is critical** — advertising, where every second of loading costs money.
- **Several languages with correct markup** as the basis of your promotion.
- **The site will grow** and you want that growth not to hit a template.
- **Nobody will maintain it** and you need a solution that does not fall apart unattended.

## How to decide in five minutes

Answer four questions:

1. **Who will change the content?** If you, and often, you need a panel — an argument for WordPress or for a custom solution with editable sections.
2. **Do you have a requirement others do not?** If so, you will outgrow a template.
3. **Who will maintain the site?** If nobody, a custom solution is more reliable.
4. **What will things look like in two years?** If the site stays the same, take the cheaper option. If you plan to grow, budget for the rebuild.
